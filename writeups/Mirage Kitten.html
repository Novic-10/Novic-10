Executive Summary & Overview

This writeup details an investigation into a suspicious file hash (A239E655709A2518DD0B7BDBED163679) identified during a retrospective sweep on a workstation belonging to Karim Adel, a maintenance planning engineer at Meridian Nile Aviation Services. The file itself was no longer present, prompting a threat intelligence investigation to identify the tooling, associated threat actor, and potential follow-up hunting activities.

The analysis focused on open-source intelligence (OSINT) to correlate the hash with known malware families and threat actors, specifically Mirage Kitten (also known as UNC1549, NightLedger, Smoke Sandstorm, Screening Serpens, and Nimbus Manticore). The investigation uncovered details about their command and control (C2) infrastructure, malware variants like NightLedger and TwoStroke, and specific TTPs, including phishing for initial access and the use of websockets for covert network access.

Key Takeaways & Security Insights

  • •
    Threat Actor Naming Conventions: Understanding how different vendors (e.g., Microsoft, Palo Alto Networks, MITRE) name threat actors is crucial for effective threat intelligence. Microsoft's naming convention, for instance, provides a structured approach to tracking and communicating about threat groups.
  • •
    Importance of OSINT: Publicly available reports and articles from various security vendors (Kaspersky, Technadu, Broadcom, Google Cloud, Group-IB, The Hacker News) are invaluable for correlating IOCs, understanding TTPs, and identifying threat actor aliases.
  • •
    Seed Reports vs. Follow-up Publications: Differentiating between initial "seed reports" that establish baselines (IOCs, TTPs, initial tracking names) and later "follow-up publications" that provide full context, campaign history, and alias mapping is essential for comprehensive threat analysis. Seed reports often serve as the originating source in threat intelligence portals, while follow-ups are listed as references.
  • •
    Malware Evolution: Threat actors like Mirage Kitten continuously evolve their toolkits, as seen with the progression from TwoStroke to NightLedger. This necessitates ongoing monitoring and analysis of new samples and their C2 mechanisms.
  • •
    Infrastructure Analysis: Mapping C2 infrastructure, identifying dominant Autonomous Systems (ASNs), and pinpointing exposed management panels (e.g., 3x-ui) are critical for network defense and proactive blocking.
  • •
    Cross-Platform Capabilities: The emergence of cross-platform malware families (e.g., PollCat) and their unique behaviors (like using HTTP status 400 for success) highlight the need for diverse detection strategies.
